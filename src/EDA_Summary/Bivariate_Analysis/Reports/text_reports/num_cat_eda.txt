* **Overall agreement** – For Age, Quantity, Unit Price, Discount, Delivery Days the two approaches reach the same conclusion (no significant difference).  
* **Discrepancies** –  
  * **Rating**: Welch’s t‑test (p ≈ 0.06) suggests a possible difference, whereas Mann‑Whitney U (p ≈ 0.11) does not. The effect is small (≈ 0.03 rating points).  
  * **Total Amount**: Mann‑Whitney U (p ≈ 0.053) hints at a higher spend for returned orders, while the Welch t‑test (p ≈ 0.37) finds no significant difference. The mean difference (~ 649) represents roughly a 5 % lift, which could be practically relevant despite the statistical ambiguity.  

Overall, the parametric and non‑parametric tests are consistent for most variables; the only notable divergence lies in the marginal significance of Rating and Total Amount.

### 4. Practical significance (effect‑size perspective)  

*Effect‑size metrics are not supplied in the workbook; consequently, quantitative assessment of practical importance (e.g., Cohen’s d, η²) cannot be performed.  

Nevertheless, the **magnitude of observed differences** can be examined:

* **Rating** – The largest observed mean gap (Male 3.87 vs. Other 3.85) is ≈ 0.02 points on a 5‑point scale (< 0.5 % of the scale range). This is unlikely to have a material business impact.  
* **Total Amount** – The mean difference between returned (13,136) and not‑returned (12,487) orders is ≈ 649, i.e., about **5 % of the mean total spend**. If the returned‑order cohort represents a meaningful customer segment (e.g., high‑value purchasers), the commercial impact could be non‑trivial despite the borderline p‑value.  
* **Unit Price** – Female customers pay roughly **2 %** more per unit than males (7,510 vs. 7,331). Given the volume of transactions, this translates into a measurable revenue contribution.  

In the absence of formal effect‑size values, the above qualitative estimates are the best indication of practical relevance.

### 5. Key business implications & patterns  

| Insight | Business implication |
|---------|----------------------|
| **Gender‑linked pricing** – Female customers have higher average unit prices, yet similar purchase quantities. This suggests a potential upside: targeting female shoppers with premium‑priced or higher‑margin items could increase revenue. |
| **City‑level price variation** – Bengaluru and Hyderabad command the highest unit prices, while Guntur and other Tier‑2 cities show lower prices. Pricing strategies may need to be regionalised; higher prices in metro‑type cities could be justified by higher disposable income or lower price sensitivity. |
| **Product‑category revenue drivers** – Electronics and Home & Kitchen categories generate the largest total amounts (≈ 39 M and ≈ 23 M respectively) and also exhibit high unit prices. These categories merit focused inventory, promotion, and margin‑optimization efforts. |
| **Return behaviour** – While return rates do not differ significantly by age, gender, or city, returned orders show a modestly higher total amount and slightly higher rating scores. This may indicate that **higher‑value or higher‑satisfaction purchases are more likely to be returned** (e.g., fit‑issues, change‑of‑mind). Monitoring return reasons and correlating them with product type and price could reduce return rates. |
| **Delivery time heterogeneity** – Certain products (Cookware Set, Hair Dryer, Shoes) and cities (Guntur, Hyderabad) have higher average delivery days. Lengthy delivery cycles can erode customer satisfaction and increase logistics costs; targeted process improvements or localized fulfillment hubs may be warranted. |
| **Customer‑lifetime value (CLV)** – The “Other” gender segment, despite being a small sample (n = 439), commands the highest total amount (≈ 14 M) and unit price, suggesting a niche high‑value customer base. Tailored marketing or loyalty programs for this segment could amplify revenue. |
| **Age stability** – Age distributions are remarkably consistent across gender, city, product, and payment method. Age is not a driver of any observed behavioural differences, implying that age‑based segmentation may have limited ROI. |
| **Quantity uniformity** – Median quantity per order is 2 across all demographic groups, indicating a standard purchase basket size. Variations are minimal, so upselling or bundling strategies should focus on product mix rather than order frequency. |

**Overall take‑away:** The data reveal clear **price and spend differentials** across gender, city, and product categories, while **behavioural differences (returns, ratings, delivery days) are subtle and only marginally statistically significant**. Business actions should therefore prioritize **pricing optimisation, targeted marketing to high‑value segments, logistics optimisation for high‑delivery‑time items, and close monitoring of returned‑order patterns** rather than age‑based or broad demographic campaigns.
