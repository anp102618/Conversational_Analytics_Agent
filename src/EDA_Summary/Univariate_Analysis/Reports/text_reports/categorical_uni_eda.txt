### 3. Rare categories / long‑tail distributions  

* **Product** – The tail is long: the top 10 products account for ~21 % of all product records, while the remaining 25 product values each represent <5 % of the total, with several (e.g., *Data Science Book*, *Novel*, *Yoga Mat*) below 3 % each.  
* **City** – The top 10 cities each represent ~8–9 % of records; the remaining 2 cities (not listed in the top‑10) together make up <2 % of the data, indicating a long‑tail of smaller urban markets.  
* **Payment_Method** – The six methods are evenly spread (15–19 % each); no pronounced long‑tail, but **COD** (15.86 %) is the least common method.  
* **Returned** – The “Yes” class (12.21 %) is a clear minority; within this class, individual return reasons (if any) are not captured, but the class itself is rare relative to the overall dataset.  

*Implication*: Features with many low‑frequency categories (especially **Product**) may benefit from **grouping** (e.g., clustering similar products, creating “Other” buckets) or **frequency‑based encoding** to avoid sparsity in downstream models.

---

### 4. Data quality concerns  

* **Missing values** – All six categorical features have **0 missing counts**; the dataset is fully populated for these variables.  
* **Sparse coverage** – No missingness, but **Product** (30 unique values) and **City** (12 unique values) have relatively low **unique_percentage** (0.20 % and 0.08 % respectively), meaning each category is well‑represented.  
* **Class imbalance** – The **Returned** target variable is heavily imbalanced (87.8 % “No”), which could affect model performance if not addressed (e.g., via resampling or class‑weighting).  

Overall, data quality appears high regarding completeness; the primary concern is the **imbalance** in the target and the **high cardinality** of the **Product** feature.
